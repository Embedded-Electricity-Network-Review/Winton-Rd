About this page

Published by the Owners Corporation Committee, 5/5A Winton Road, Malvern East VIC. Last updated 12 November 2026.

In short

The committee is ending Shine Power's contract because Shine charges the highest price the law allows, will not explain the building's solar, ignored every letter, and holds a contract that otherwise renews itself until 2031 and beyond. Owners agreed in September 2026: 78% of lot entitlements voted to end it.

26 June 2026 was the rollover cut-off

The contract rolls over automatically every five years. To stop the rollover due on 26 December 2026, notice had to be given by 26 June 2026. Without it, the contract would have run to December 2031.

Shine Power response

Since March 2026, Shine has not answered a single letter, email or phone call from the committee or the manager. It also did not respond to a complaint lodged with the Energy Ombudsman. Shine's only replies have come from its lawyers, who claim that a 99-year lease over two plant areas and the roof entitles Shine to stay.

Discovery of a 99-year lease

Shine believes it holds a 99-year lease, signed in December 2016 for $1 rent, over two plant areas and the roof where the solar panels sit. The developer owned every lot at that time. There is no record of this lease in any AGM minutes, or in the contracts of sale owners signed when buying from the developer. Shine's lawyers first mentioned it in a letter dated 23 June 2026, which reached the OC's lawyers on 29 July 2026. A lease has been provided by their lawyers however the validity can not be confirmed.

Legal costs are higher than first estimated

That is mainly because of the lease Shine produced in August. The $30,000 special levy pays invoices already received and the next stage of work.

The levy is within the committee's legal power

A special levy needs a vote of owners only if it is more than twice the annual fees.

Origin is cheaper

A switch to Origin cuts electricity usage rates by about 20% and pays the OC $2,500 a year for five years.

Why end Shine's contract

The reasons are specific, not just a general worry about value. The committee began reviewing the network in November 2024. It reached these findings at its 13 April 2026 meeting:

ConcernWhat the committee found
PriceShine charges the highest price allowed by law for embedded networks, the Victorian Default Offer. That is about 28.8c per kWh plus $1.16 a day.
SolarThe building has rooftop solar, but the OC has no records showing who owns it, who maintains it, or who receives the credits it earns. Shine would not answer these questions.
No responseShine did not answer the committee's letter of 13 March 2026, the follow-up of 9 April, or earlier enquiries in May and July 2025. Shine told the manager it would not respond.
Contract termsThe developer signed the contract in 2016, before most owners bought. It renews itself, and the OC can leave only at set times and only with a special resolution. Shine can leave on 90 days' notice.

The committee's view is that owners should not be locked in for decades to a provider that charges the maximum and will not account for the building's own solar.

Why the Ombudsman could not solve this on its own. EWOV handles customer complaints about service and billing. It cannot end a contract, decide whether a lease is valid, or rule that contract terms are unfair. Only the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT) or a court can do that, which is why EWOV pointed the committee to legal advice.

Why lawyers are involved, and how they were chosen

Lawyers became necessary once Shine ignored the notice and then disputed it through its own lawyers. Without legal steps, the contract would simply have continued to 2031.

  • 16 June 2026: the OC's lawyers advised that the termination notice was validly served. They recommended a special resolution of owners, so that Shine could not argue the termination was ineffective.
  • 10 July 2026: a letter of demand was sent to Shine.
  • 28 July to August 2026: Shine's lawyers chased their 23 June letter, which said a 99-year lease, signed by the developer in December 2016 for $1 rent, gives Shine the right to keep its equipment in two plant areas and on the roof. The OC had no copy of this lease until Shine's lawyers provided one in August. Reviewing it, and answering Shine's lawyers, is the main reason costs rose.
  • 11 October 2026: the OC's lawyers wrote to Shine's lawyers and proposed a meeting to settle the matter.

Origin's offer cuts the electricity usage rate by about 20% and pays the OC $2,500 a year. It also gives the OC back control over solar, batteries and EV charging.

Savings for residents. An apartment using 2,500 kWh a year would pay about $145 a year less for usage, or about $4,500 a year across 31 apartments. This is an estimate; actual use varies.

Choice. The OC would no longer be locked into one provider. The Victorian Government has also committed to giving embedded network customers access to competitive retail offers, and the OC needs to be free to take that up.

Solar. A new arrangement would make clear who owns the rooftop panels and who receives the benefit. Origin can review the panels' condition (about $1,300), which the committee has put off until ownership is known.

Batteries and EV charging. Under the current contract, the OC cannot let any other provider access the network equipment without Shine's consent, and Shine also claims a lease over the plant areas and roof. Ending the arrangement lets the OC look at shared batteries to cut the building's running costs, and at EV charging for residents. Neither is part of Origin's offer yet.

A properly registered provider. In Victoria, a business selling electricity through a building's network to 10 or more homes must register with the Essential Services Commission, join EWOV and keep prices at or below the legal maximum. EWOV lists Shine's membership as "The Trustee for the Shine Power Unit Trust", while the OC's contract and lease are with Shine Power Pty Ltd. Origin is a licensed national retailer that already runs the building's hot water network.

Why a special levy

Legal costs were not in the 2026 budget. Potential legal costs were raised at the 2026 AGM with a resolution to raise a special levy for the cost. On 12 October the OC had $23,794 in the bank, with about $16,000 of legal invoices and $5,000 of contractor bills due. That would have left about $2,800 for normal running costs and the January insurance renewal.

The levy

$30,000 is raised in four instalments of $7,500, due 11 December 2026, 9 January, 14 February and 15 March 2027. Each lot pays according to its lot liability, about $970 per lot over the four instalments on average.

Is there a limit?

Yes. Under section 24 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006, a special levy must be based on lot liability. It needs a special resolution of owners if it is more than twice the current annual fees. The annual fees here are $122,500, so the threshold is about $245,000. Below that, the committee can decide a levy for unusual expenses itself. This levy is about a quarter of one year's fees.

If the matter goes to VCAT, more money would be needed. Owners would be told the estimate before any such step.

Insurance

The committee has told the OC's insurance broker about the dispute, and the broker was not concerned. If Shine starts legal proceedings, the committee will notify the insurer in writing straight away, as policies require.

What happens next

The aim is a negotiated exit before 26 December 2026, without going to VCAT.

  1. The OC's lawyers have written to Shine's lawyers and proposed a meeting to settle the matter.
  2. Origin needs about three months' notice to take over the network, and is ready to do so.
  3. The committee will check every legal invoice before it is paid, and will ask for a written estimate before any new stage of work.
  4. If Shine will not agree, the committee will report the options and their likely cost to owners before taking any step at VCAT.
